      Places and people

      Sub-district (tehsil, taluka, mandal)
      The administrative level below the district. The atlas uses 6,820 sub-districts from geoBoundaries (Local Government Directory, 2018), so names and borders may differ from later notifications.
      People exposed
      Residents in 2020 whose home grid cell lies in the hazard area. Exposure is not damage: it does not reflect housing quality, income, warning systems or local defences.
      GPW and GHS-POP
      Two 2020 population grids at about 1 km. GPW spreads each census unit's count evenly over its area. GHS-POP places people where satellites see buildings, so it follows settlements and is usually the better guide on floodplains. Choose between them in the Findings tab.
      Projected population (SSP)
      Population by 2030–2090 following each SSP's storyline of births, deaths, migration and urbanisation, mapped at 1 km by Wang et al. (2022). The atlas applies each sub-district's projected growth to its 2020 count, and for flood zones the growth of people inside the zone, so growth on floodplains counts. Future flood-zone figures use it unless they say "2020 population".
      Lakh and crore
      1 lakh = 100,000. 1 crore = 10 million (100 lakh).

      Flood hazard today

      Flood susceptibility
      How prone land is to flooding given its terrain, nearness to rivers, soils, land cover and rainfall climate. It shows where floods tend to occur, not when, how deep or how often.
      GFSM
      Global Flood Susceptibility Map v1 (Waleed et al., 2026): a 30 m map built from satellite and geospatial data that rates land in five classes. It is the basis of today's figures in this atlas.
      Flood-prone land (High+)
      Land in GFSM's High or Very High classes, the top two of five (Very Low, Low, Moderate, High, Very High).
      1-in-100-year flood (return period)
      A flood with a 1% chance of happening in any given year. It is not "once a century": over 30 years the chance of at least one is about 26%. A larger return period means a rarer, bigger flood.
      Flood zone
      Land that WRI Aqueduct Floods models as under water in a 1-in-100-year river or coastal flood, at about 1 km, with no flood defences. A cell counts if any depth is modelled. Future coastal maps include median sea-level rise and land subsidence.
      Sea-level rise
      The projected rise of mean sea level relative to the land, against 1995–2014, from the IPCC's Sixth Assessment (AR6). The atlas uses the nearest point on AR6's 1° grid; local land sinking measured at tide gauges is left out because the estimates disagree sharply, so delta areas may see more.
      Land below sea level
      Land whose ground height (30 m DeltaDTM model) is below today's or the projected mean sea level. A simple "bathtub" estimate: it ignores whether the land connects to the sea, and much of it is already behind embankments, as in the Sundarbans and Kuttanad. Read it as land that depends on protection and drainage, not land that will flood. Tides and storm surges come on top; see Flood zone.
      Land subsidence
      Sinking ground, from groundwater pumping and the settling of delta sediments. The coastal flood-zone maps include it, which is part of why low-lying delta areas such as greater Kolkata show large future increases.
      Flood depth
      Modelled water depth above ground during the flood, in metres. Over 1 m is generally dangerous to people and buildings.
      Flood defences
      Embankments, levees, sea walls and dams. The flood-zone maps leave them out, so in protected areas they show the hazard if defences fail or are overtopped, an upper bound.
      River and coastal flooding
      River (fluvial) floods come from rivers overtopping their banks after heavy or prolonged rain upstream. Coastal floods come from storm surges and high tides, made worse by sea-level rise.
      Flash and urban flooding
      Intense local rain that overwhelms drains and small streams. The flood-zone maps do not cover it; the wettest-day rainfall indicator is the closest guide here.

      Climate projections

      Emissions scenario (SSP, RCP)
      A storyline of future greenhouse-gas emissions. The current SSPs (CMIP6) replaced the older RCPs (CMIP5); SSP2-4.5 is roughly RCP4.5. Here SSP1-2.6 is low, SSP2-4.5 middle, SSP3-7.0 high and SSP5-8.5 very high. Each SSP also has its own population path (see Projected population). Flood-zone maps exist only for RCP4.5 and RCP8.5, so the atlas shows RCP4.5 for SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5, and RCP8.5 for SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5.
      Period and baseline
      Rainfall changes are 20-year averages (2020–39 to 2080–99) compared with 1995–2014. Flood-zone maps are centred on 2030, 2050 and 2080 and compared with a 1960–1999 baseline; 2060–79 and 2080–99 both use the 2080 map.
      Climate model
      A computer simulation of the global climate. Rainfall projections use about 30 CMIP6 models; flood-zone maps use 5 CMIP5 models. Models differ, so the atlas shows the middle value (median) and a range.
      Model range
      For rainfall, the 10th to 90th percentile across models: 8 in 10 models fall inside it. For flood zones, the lowest to highest of the 5 models.
      Models agree
      At least 90% of models project an increase. Where they don't, the direction of change is uncertain.
      Wettest 5-day rainfall (Rx5day)
      The year's largest rainfall total over 5 consecutive days, averaged over the period. Linked to river flooding.
      Wettest day (Rx1day)
      The year's largest single-day rainfall, averaged over the period. Linked to flash and urban flooding.
      Flood pressure index
      Share of flood-prone land × (1 + change in wettest 5-day rainfall). It ranks where heavier rain meets flood-prone land. It is a screening index, not a probability.

      Reading the map

      Relative and absolute classes
      Relative (quantile) classes put the same number of sub-districts in each colour, good for ranking. Absolute (fixed) classes use the same thresholds everywhere, good for comparing states.
      Screening, not forecasting
      The atlas is for comparing places and setting priorities for closer study. It is not suited to decisions about individual sites, which need local flood studies.

      Flood susceptibility, and the people living with it, for 6,820 tehsils, talukas and mandals, summarised from the 30 m Global Flood Susceptibility Map (GFSM v1).

      It answers three questions for each sub-district: how much land is flood-prone today, how many people live there or in the 1-in-100-year flood zone, and how extreme rainfall and flood zones may change under each emissions scenario.

      Start with the Findings tab for headline numbers, click any sub-district for its profile, and use the Glossary for terms. All figures are for screening and comparison, not forecasts for individual sites.
